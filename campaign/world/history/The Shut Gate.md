Before there was a Nordheim there were farmers on the cold side of the
[[Stoneborn Holds]], and they were not there by choice.

A hold is a city with no fields. Highforge and the deep cities under it could
cut steel but not grow oats. So the valleys on the mountain's northern skirts
were worked for them, by people the Assembly's records call the field-holdings
and never call anything else. They were cattle-keepers and oat-growers who
fostered their children between families and kept their dead in praise-songs.
Half of Nordheim's given names sound nothing like the other half because of it.

The arrangement lasted centuries and was not slavery in any thane's telling.
The quota was set at the Assembly and rose in bad years, because bad years
were when the holds needed most, and a family that missed it lost the holding.
It was all written down. It is still in the ledgers, in a clear hand.

## The winter of 1124

It came early and it did not break. The oats had already failed. By the turn
of the year the northern valleys were burning fence-rails and eating seed
corn, and the field-holdings went up the road to Highforge and asked to be let
in until spring.

The gates stayed shut.

They stayed shut for eleven weeks. What the Assembly said at the time is
recorded and is not unreasonable: the holds were on short commons themselves,
and the grain that would have fed the farmers was the grain keeping
Highforge's furnace crews alive. The thanes debated it properly and voted,
and they wrote down that they had.

Nobody knows how many died on the road. Nordheim's count is one in three and
Nordheim has never had any way to check it.

## The going north

What came down off that mountain in the spring was not farmers. They took
what they needed from the first steadings they reached and kept going, north
and then east along the coast. For something like a generation they were
nothing anyone would call a people — a moving column that fed itself by
arriving. They got good at it fast.

They reached the fjords in about 1150 and found them held.

## The Jotun

The [[Jotunborn]] clans of the far north had been there beyond any reckoning,
fishing the cold water and hunting the ice and keeping to themselves so
thoroughly that most of the continent took them for a rumour rather than a
people. They were not gentle and they were not warlike. They had never met
anything worth fighting, and the north had made them hard enough that nothing
had tried twice.

They took the column in. The sagas are unhelpfully brief about why. The first
winter, the clans fed a starving people they had no reason to feed. The
incomers had been told for two hundred years that this did not happen.

Then each side taught the other what it had.

The Jotun gave the sea: which water fishes, which ice holds, where the whale
run turns, how to be out in weather that kills. Everything Nordheim is famous
for on the water it learned in a clan boat.

The incomers gave the forge and the war. They had two centuries of watching
dwarven metallurgy from the wrong side of it, and had gone hungry rather than
stupid: they arrived knowing how to make steel, move men in ranks, and hold
ground by agreement rather than argument. The Jotun took all three, and
raiding suited them at once.

Within four generations you could not sort the two by their habits, only by
their height.

The names went the same way and went further. The clans already spoke the
hard northern tongue that gives Nordheim its patronymics, so a stranger who
assumes the whole Norse half of the country is the human half has it
backwards — the incomers brought the softer names, and what they found
waiting was Ketilsdóttir and Bjarnsson. The two stocks never sorted
themselves out. A Gaelic given name on a Jotun patronymic raises no eyebrow,
and half the fjords are crewed by men called things like Ronan Hravnsson
without anyone hearing two languages in it.

## The Thing

The government came out of the same seam. Jotun clans had no chiefs. They
kept key roles — chroniclers, keepers, scouts — and when a decision was
needed the seniors of those roles met and voted. A senior outmatched by a
younger hand stepped down to apprentice without anyone thinking it strange.
The incomers had the opposite inheritance: heritable rank, sworn obligation,
and a clear notion of who answers to whom, passing from a father to whoever
he had got.

The Great Thing at Hravnfjord in c. 1180 is those two things bolted together,
and it still shows the join. Rank is heritable and held for life — chief,
jarl and king alike — which came up the road with the incomers. The assembly
can overrule any jarl including the High King, which came out of the clans.
That is why the High King's authority stops where the Thing says it does and
never creeps.

The seam runs right through the crown. Every seat in Nordheim passes from
father to heir except the one at the top, which is filled by a vote and was
never anybody's to leave. A dead High King's son inherits his father's
jarldom and nothing more, and stands at the next Thing on the same footing as
every other jarl in the hall. Houses have held the crown three reigns running
on the strength of being worth electing. None has ever held it by right. The
clans who made that rule had spent their whole history watching seniors step
down when a younger hand outmatched them.

Even the word for the lowest rung is borrowed. Nordheim's chiefs hold towns,
and the clans that had never had a chief in their lives took the title for a
job they had never needed until they were living in towns worth holding.

**What did not survive the welding was any notion of whose seat is whose.**
Nordheim has one government and has never had two. A jarl is a jarl,
inherited on the same terms in every jarldom, and eleven of the twenty-six
High Kings crowned since c. 1520 have been Jotun. Nobody in the fjords keeps
that count. A Corvane pamphleteer worked it out and found it remarkable. The
Thing's own rolls don't record ancestry at all, because no procedure has ever
needed to ask.

## What is remembered

The far northern jarldoms are mostly Jotun and the southern ones mostly
human, a fact about where the cold is and nothing else. They have been
marrying, crewing and voting together for nine centuries. A northern jarl
speaks for the humans in his jarldom and a southern one for the Jotun in his,
and neither half regards the other as incomers. Ask which, and you'll be told
the question is stupid.

Two things in Nordheim make no sense without this and are never explained with
it. The first is [[Cayden Cailean]], god of bravery, freedom, and the
guest-cup — a national creed that comes down to *you open the door*, kept by a
people descended from those who were left outside one. The second is that
hospitality in the north is not a courtesy but a law with teeth, and a jarl
who turns away a traveller in winter can be brought before the Thing for it.

The [[Stoneborn Holds]] and Nordheim are **allied**, and have been for
centuries; the iron road between them is worth a great deal to both. The
Holds consider the matter closed and closed honourably. The ledgers are open
to anyone who asks and no thane has ever pretended otherwise. Nordheim has
never asked for an apology, which the thanes have sometimes mistaken for the
thing being forgotten.

It is not forgotten. It is in a song every child in the fjords knows, and the
song is not about dwarves. It is about a door.
