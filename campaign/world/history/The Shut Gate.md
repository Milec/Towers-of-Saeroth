Before there was a Nordheim there were farmers on the cold side of the
[[Stoneborn Holds]], and they were not there by choice.

A hold is a city with no fields. Highforge and the deep cities under it
could cut steel out of the rock and could not grow a sack of oats, so the
valleys on the mountain's northern skirts were worked for them, by people the
Assembly's records call the field-holdings and never call anything else. They
were cattle-keepers and oat-growers who fostered their children between
families and kept their dead in praise-songs, and they are the reason half of
Nordheim's given names sound nothing like the other half.

The arrangement lasted centuries and was not slavery in any thane's telling.
The quota was set at the Assembly, the quota went up in bad years because bad
years were when the holds needed most, and a family that missed it lost the
holding. Everything about that was written down. It is still in the ledgers,
in a clear hand.

## The winter of 1124

It came early and it did not break. The oats had already failed. By the turn
of the year the northern valleys were burning fence-rails and eating seed
corn, and the field-holdings went up the road to Highforge and asked to be let
in until spring.

The gates stayed shut.

They stayed shut for eleven weeks. What the Assembly said at the time is
recorded and is not unreasonable: the holds were on short commons themselves,
a hold that opens its gate to a valley is a hold that starves in the dark
alongside it, and the grain that would have fed the farmers was the grain
keeping Highforge's furnace crews alive. The thanes debated it properly and
voted, and they wrote down that they had.

Nobody knows how many died on the road. Nordheim's count is one in three and
Nordheim has never had any way to check it.

## The going north

What came down off that mountain in the spring was not farmers. They took
what they needed from the first steadings they reached and kept going, north
and then east along the coast, and for something like a generation they were
nothing anyone would call a people — a moving column that fed itself by
arriving. They were very good at it quite quickly, which is its own kind of
sentence about them.

They reached the fjords in about 1150 and found them held.

## The Jotun

The [[Jotunborn]] clans of the far north had been there beyond any reckoning,
fishing the cold water and hunting the ice and keeping to themselves so
thoroughly that most of the continent did not know they were a people rather
than a rumour. They were not gentle and they were not warlike. They had simply
never met anything worth fighting, and the north had made them hard enough
that nothing had tried twice.

They took the column in. The sagas are unhelpfully brief about why. What they
record is that the first winter the clans fed a starving people they had no
reason to feed, which the incomers had been told for two hundred years was the
sort of thing that did not happen.

Then each side taught the other what it had.

The Jotun gave the sea: which water fishes, which ice holds, where the whale
run turns, how to be out in weather that kills. Everything Nordheim is famous
for on the water it learned in a clan boat.

The incomers gave the forge and the war. They had two centuries of watching
dwarven metallurgy from the wrong side of it and had gone hungry rather than
stupid, and they arrived knowing how to make steel, how to move men in ranks,
and how to hold ground by agreement rather than by argument. The Jotun took
all three, and took to raiding the way a people takes to a thing it turns out
to have been shaped for.

Within four generations you could not sort the two by their habits, only by
their height.

The names went the same way and went further. The clans already spoke the
hard northern tongue that gives Nordheim its patronymics, so a stranger who
assumes the whole Norse half of the country is the human half has it exactly
backwards — the incomers brought the softer names, and what they found waiting
was Ketilsdóttir and Bjarnsson. The two stocks never sorted themselves out.
A Gaelic given name on a Jotun patronymic raises no eyebrow at all, and half
the fjords are crewed by men called things like Ronan Hravnsson without anyone
hearing two languages in it.

## The Thing

The government came out of the same seam. Jotun clans had no chiefs. They kept
key roles — chroniclers, keepers, scouts — and when a decision was needed the
seniors of those roles met and voted, and a senior who was outmatched by a
younger hand stepped down to apprentice without anyone thinking it strange.
The incomers had the opposite inheritance: fixed rank, sworn obligation, and a
clear notion of who answers to whom.

The Great Thing at Hravnfjord in c. 1180 is those two things bolted together
and it still shows the join. Jarls hold rank and hold it for life, which came
up the road with the incomers. The assembly can overrule any jarl including
the High King, which came out of the clans, and it is why the High King's
authority stops exactly where the Thing says it does and has never once been
allowed to creep.

**What did not survive the welding was any notion of whose seat is whose.**
Nordheim has one government and it has never had two. A jarl is a jarl,
elected or inherited on the same terms in every jarldom, and eleven of the
twenty-six High Kings crowned since c. 1520 have been Jotun. Nobody in the
fjords keeps that count. It was worked out by a Corvane pamphleteer who found
it remarkable, and the Thing's own rolls do not record ancestry at all,
because no procedure has ever needed to ask.

## What is remembered

The far northern jarldoms are mostly Jotun and the southern ones mostly human,
which is a fact about where the cold is and about nothing else. They have been
marrying, crewing and voting together for nine centuries, a northern jarl
speaks for the humans in his jarldom and a southern one for the Jotun in his,
and neither half regards the other as incomers. Ask which, and you will be
told the question is stupid.

Two things in Nordheim make no sense without this and are never explained with
it. The first is [[Cayden Cailean]], god of bravery, freedom, and the
guest-cup — a national creed that comes down to *you open the door*, kept by a
people descended from those who were left outside one. The second is that
hospitality in the north is not a courtesy but a law with teeth, and a jarl
who turns away a traveller in winter can be brought before the Thing for it.

The [[Stoneborn Holds]] and Nordheim are **allied**, and have been for
centuries, and the iron road between them is worth a great deal to both. The
Holds consider the matter closed and closed honourably; the ledgers are open
to anyone who asks and no thane has ever pretended otherwise. Nordheim has
never asked for an apology, which the thanes have occasionally mistaken for
the thing being forgotten.

It is not forgotten. It is in a song every child in the fjords knows, and the
song is not about dwarves. It is about a door.
