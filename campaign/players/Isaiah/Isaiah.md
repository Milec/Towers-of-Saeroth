## Appearance and manner

Victarion stands 6 feet 5 inches tall, lean and athletic, with near-white
platinum-blond hair. His left eye is gold and ringed like a sun; his right
eye is blood-red, with a pupil that narrows to a slit. An old wound runs
across his throat, with missing flesh and smaller cuts suggesting a
struggle. The draft does not yet describe how he received it.

He is usually quiet or stoic with strangers, becoming more eager and jovial
with people he knows. When hunger grows strong, his lips seldom conceal his
fangs. Gold thread from his mother's veil is sewn into his cuff. It is worn
and fraying, and the draft describes it as a promise not to feed.

## Backstory so far

The submitted draft is titled *Lumen et Sanguis*, “Light and Blood,” and is
arranged as a triptych. Its central concern is Victarion's hope to become
more than blood and destiny demand of him. The written narrative currently
covers his birth and first three winters; later sections are unfinished.

### Annunciation: the conception

Victarion's father was a duke whose only son had died four winters earlier,
aged seven. Seeking another heir, he married a fiercely devout woman from
the highlands near his estate. Their bond rested on faith and the desire
for a child more than love.

A curved blade was uncovered beneath his lands, clean despite its years in
red soil. Its whispers fed the duke's vision of a son born through blood
and magic. When his wife recognized the danger and tried to flee, he
imprisoned her. Clerics prepared for the birth and ordered a month of prayer
through the citadels and churches.

Although the pregnancy had lasted only eight months, the ritual's
attendants insisted the child had to be born that night beneath the full
moon. The duke raised the blade against his wife. She seized it, stabbed
his eye, cut his throat, and escaped in the confusion as the guards divided
between them. Those who helped her reach a horse later paid with their
lives. The draft subsequently refers to the duke as surviving and receiving
Brother Marcus; his eventual fate is not supplied.

Bleeding and pursued by the clergy, she reached a shallow cavern in the
duke's woods. She used the blade to deliver the premature child herself,
praying for him to survive. At the river she found a pristine golden cradle
with a soft pillow and understood it as an answer to her prayer.

Brother Marcus followed her cries. He saw a winged humanoid keeping watch
over mother and child, raising a finger to forbid him from approaching.
Victarion's mother laid him in the cradle, severed the cord, and died beside
the river.

### The cradle

After her death, the winged figure allowed Marcus forward. He chose not to
return the infant to the duke. He covered the child's brow with his mother's
golden veil, set the cradle in the current, and took the blade beneath his
robes.

Marcus died after weeks of torture on returning to his liege. The draft
records a rumor that he buried the blade and carried its location to his
grave; it leaves his precise actions for the GM to decide.

Before leaving, the winged guardian touched the infant and prolonged his
life. Tiny feathered wings appeared on his back. The cradle travelled
downriver into the forest of a distant land.

### Baba

A young cauthooj that had recently lost its clutch found the infant and
took him into its nest. It sheltered him from the weather, protected him
from predators, and brought a nursing goat to feed him until he could eat
other food. Victarion came to call the bird “Baba.”

They spent three winters together. His small wings began to move as he
learned to walk. Then villagers from a wealthy merchant's estate came into
the woods seeking water for new farmland. They found the child and believed
him lost. Baba fought to protect him; the villagers did not understand what
they were seeing.

The fight left villagers dead and Baba wounded by their spears. Victarion
was carried back toward the village, reaching and crying for the creature
that had raised him. The written account leaves Baba curled around its
wounds without explicitly confirming its death.
