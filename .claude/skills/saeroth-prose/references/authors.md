# Three writers, and what to actually steal

Read the section you need; there is no reason to read all three at once.

- [Martin](#martin) — point of view, the concrete detail, institutions
- [Tolkien](#tolkien) — depth by implication, register, land and names
- [Sanderson](#sanderson) — rules, cost, consequence, in-world documents
- [Choosing between them](#choosing-between-them)
- [Borrowing badly](#borrowing-badly)

The point is never to imitate a voice. It is that each of them solved a
specific problem that GM notes have too, and their solution is portable.

---

## Martin

**The problem he solves:** how to make a setting feel inhabited rather than
described.

### Nothing is neutral

Martin almost never states a fact about the world in the narrator's voice. It
arrives through somebody who has a stake in it, and their stake bends it. A
maester, a knight and a servant walking into the same hall notice three
different rooms.

In a GM note you rarely have a POV character, but you have the same lever:
attribute the claim. Compare —

> The Cathedral Court's rite is not valid outside Dalstan.

> Dalstan holds that its rite is the valid one, and has never retracted it.
> Everyone else calls that the same move it made four centuries ago.

The first is a ruling. The second is a fight the party can walk into. Any
sentence a nation would argue with should say who is making it.

### The specific over the sufficient

Martin's world is built out of prices, dishes, smells and heraldry. Lemon
cakes. The particular way a sword is worn. This is not decoration — it is how
he makes a scene feel physically real without describing the room.

For notes: reach for the price, the count, the hour, the dish, the maker's
mark. *Fifteen thousand head a year* is worth more than *a major horse trade*
and costs the same number of words.

### Institutions have inertia and appetite

Councils, orders and banks in Martin behave like organisms that want to keep
existing. They are slow, they protect themselves, and they act from interest
rather than from morality. That is why his politics feel real and why a
"council of wise elders who do the right thing" never does.

Ask of any body in a note: what does it want, what is it afraid of, what would
it do to survive, and what has it done before.

### Obliqueness

People in Martin rarely say the thing. They talk around it, and the reader
does the arithmetic. Applied to notes: an NPC's **Want** should be a want, and
their dialogue should not be it. The gap is the character.

### Rhythm

Martin cuts hard at the end. A long paragraph, then four words. The vault's
own prose does the opposite almost everywhere, which is why the
**short-sentence share** in `prose_check.py` matters.

## Tolkien

**The problem he solves:** how a world seems older and larger than the pages
that describe it.

### Depth by implication

The most-copied and least-understood thing he does. He mentions Gondolin,
Beren, the Watcher in the Water, and moves on. The reader concludes that the
world has a history, because only a real history would be mentioned that
casually and left alone.

The trick is **confidence plus no gloss**. It fails the moment you explain.
It fails again if you invent a name with no weight behind it — the referent
has to sound like it *could* be looked up, even when it cannot.

Practically, per note: one or two references to things you do not define. The
vault does this well already — the Silent Years, the thing the Cindral wardens
declined to discuss, the fourteen-line Sahenna recitation nobody can source.
Keep doing it, and resist the urge to write the note that explains them.

### Register is a map

Tolkien changes English by *who is speaking* and how old the thing is. Hobbits
talk in plain modern idiom with contractions. Rohirrim get Anglo-Saxon rhythm
and compound nouns. Gondor is Latinate, formal, longer-breathed. Charters and
prophecy get archaic syntax.

For the vault: a Quivari salon, a Stoneborn thane and a Concord liturgy should
not sound alike. The lever is vocabulary root and sentence length, not
apostrophes and *thee*. Anglo-Saxon words for physical things (*stone, blood,
road, winter, oath*), Latinate for ceremony and administration (*jurisdiction,
consecration, arbitration*).

### The land is walked, not viewed

Landscape in Tolkien is described in terms of crossing it: how long, how cold,
what it costs. That is exactly the information a GM needs and almost never what
a location note contains.

Write distances in days, passes in seasons, rivers by whether they can be
forded. *Only three passes, and the range between them is not held* is
geography a party can plan against.

### Names mean something

He built languages first, so his names decompose. You do not need a language —
you need names that *behave* like a language: derived from an event, a
landform, or an owner, with recurring elements. *Harrowgate, Grauthaven,
Brightfurrow, Ambry Ford.* If two places in the same region share a root, the
region acquires a history for free.

### Understatement at the peak

The biggest moments get the plainest words. When a note's content is genuinely
enormous — a god dying, an empire erased — the prose should get *quieter*, not
grander. Grandeur in the sentence competes with grandeur in the fact and loses.

## Sanderson

**The problem he solves:** how the fantastical stays interesting after the
first surprise, and how a world holds together under a curious player.

### Limitation over power

His actual rule is that what a magic *cannot* do is more interesting than what
it can. The same is true of institutions, orders and artefacts. A Drake Rider
Order that can go anywhere is scenery; one that will not take the field beside
paid companies has a politics.

For every capability in a note, write the boundary. Who may not use it, what it
costs, what it breaks, when it fails.

### Follow the consequence two steps

Sanderson's worlds feel engineered because he asks the second and third
question. If a nation has the only working firelock: who buys them, who is
forbidden to, what happens to the armourers' guild next door, who is trying to
steal the process, and what does that do to the price of tin.

This is the highest-yield habit in the whole skill for generating plot. Most
notes stop at the first fact.

### Rules stated plainly

His prose is deliberately transparent — it gets out of the way so the reader
can hold the mechanism. When a note is explaining how something *works*, do not
decorate it. Save the good sentences for what the mechanism means.

The towers note is the model: the threshold rule is stated flatly and at
length, because everything at the table depends on the players understanding
it exactly.

### In-world documents

Epigraphs, ledgers, letters, court minutes. A fragment of a real document
carries more conviction than any amount of description, and it comes with a
built-in author who has a bias — which is the Martin move arriving by another
road. A charter clause, an insurer's memorandum, a marginal note by a clerk
four hundred years later.

### Foreshadowing is load-bearing

Setups pay off. In a campaign vault this means a detail planted in a nation
note should be findable later — the crate's maker's mark, the reagent flask
left behind, the eleven-year silence. When you invent a detail, prefer one that
could matter.

## Choosing between them

| The note needs… | Reach for |
| --- | --- |
| To feel inhabited, political, dangerous | Martin |
| To feel old, wide, and continuous past the page | Tolkien |
| To hold up under a player who asks how it works | Sanderson |
| A faction or council | Martin's appetite-and-inertia |
| A location or journey | Tolkien's walked land |
| A magic item, order, or institution's powers | Sanderson's limits |
| Read-aloud text | Tolkien's plainness at the peak, Martin's senses |
| A history note | Tolkien for the unexplained, Martin for who disputes it |

Most good notes use two. A nation note is Sanderson's consequences wearing
Martin's attributions, with two Tolkien references nobody explains.

## Borrowing badly

Each of these fails in a recognisable way, and the failures are more common
than the successes.

- **Fake Tolkien** is archaic syntax and apostrophes in names with nothing
  underneath. *Vale of Aelth'ranor* is not depth; a hill called *Harrow*
  because of what was done on it is. If a name has no derivation you could
  state if pressed, it is decoration.
- **Fake Martin** is grimness as a substitute for stakes — mud, cruelty and
  sexual violence used as texture. The engine is *competing legitimate
  interests*, not misery. A cheerful nation with an irreconcilable claim is
  more Martin than a miserable one with none.
- **Fake Sanderson** is a system with terminology and no cost, taxonomies for
  their own sake, and worldbuilding that reads as a rulebook. If the note is
  explaining a mechanism nobody at the table will interact with, cut it.

And the general failure: doing all three at once in a single paragraph. Pick
the one that solves this note's problem.
