# Worldbuilding

Read this when inventing settlements, factions, pantheons, planar material or
plot hooks. The aim is material a GM can put in front of players this week, not
an encyclopedia they will never open.

## Anchor invention to what exists

Before inventing, check whether the setting already answers the question:

```bash
python3 scripts/lookup.py find --type deity --name "<name>"
python3 scripts/lookup.py find --type plane
python3 scripts/lookup.py find --type creature-family --name "<name>"
python3 scripts/lookup.py search "<region or organisation>"
```

Homebrew anchored to real deities, planes and creature families stays compatible
with published adventures, which matters the moment a table wants to run one.
An invented god of the forge sitting beside the existing pantheon is far more
useful than one that quietly contradicts it.

## Settlements

A settlement becomes usable once it has a reason to exist, a tension, and three
places worth visiting. Statistics and population figures are the least useful
part and can wait.

Give each one:

- **A reason.** Why here? A ford, a mine, a shrine, a border. This drives who
  lives there and who wants it.
- **A tension.** Something unresolved the players can push on — a succession,
  a shortage, a newcomer, a debt. Without this a settlement is scenery.
- **Three locations.** Somewhere to rest, somewhere to trade, and somewhere that
  is a problem. Name them and give each one person.
- **Two or three named people**, each with a want and something they will not do.

Scale detail to the players' likely engagement. A waypoint gets a sentence; the
town they will base out of for ten sessions earns the full treatment.

## Factions

Factions are engines for adventures, so build them around what they are trying
to do rather than what they are:

- a **goal** that requires action, and therefore generates events;
- a **method** that constrains how they pursue it, and creates friction;
- a **weakness** the players can exploit or that keeps them from simply winning;
- a **face** — one named person the party can actually talk to.

Two factions in tension produce more play than four sketched in isolation. If a
user wants a web of factions, ask what the party wants first, then build the
ones that stand in the way or offer help.

## Pantheons and cults

Existing deities come with edicts, anathema, domains and a favoured weapon —
check the vault before inventing, since a cleric player will need those details
to build a character.

For an invented deity, decide what behaviour it demands and what it forbids
before anything else. Those two lists do more work at the table than any amount
of cosmology, because they tell a divine character what to do when it costs them
something.

Cults are usually more useful than gods. A cult has a location, a leader, a
practice and a reason locals tolerate it — all of which are adventure material.

## Plot hooks

A hook needs a change, a stake and a way in. "There are goblins in the hills" is
setting; "the goblins have stopped raiding, and nobody knows why" is a hook,
because something has changed and someone wants to know.

Aim to give the user three hooks of different shapes when they ask: one that
rewards investigation, one that rewards force, and one that rewards talking. It
lets a GM pick the one matching their table without further negotiation.

Tie at least one hook to an existing player character's background where you
know it — a hook aimed at a specific character reliably outperforms a general
one.

## Pacing a campaign

When a user is planning more than a session, the useful questions are what the
players want to do, what the opposition wants, and what changes if the players
do nothing. That last one turns a static setting into one that generates events
on its own, which is what keeps a long campaign moving without the GM pushing
every week.
